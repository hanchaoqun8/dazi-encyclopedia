Importing Tea To Australia and Canada: Quick Standard Overview

Most guides to tea compliance focus on the EU and the United States, and for good reason — they are large, demanding markets. But if you are a small tea importer or a producer looking beyond those two, Australia, New Zealand, and Canada are worth understanding. Each has its own framework for pesticide residues and food safety.

This is a high-level overview, not a substitute for the official rules. It introduces who sets the standards in each market and what to verify. It deliberately does not quote specific numeric MRLs for tea, because those values belong in the current official texts — not in a summary article.

Why standards differ across markets

Acommon question from new exporters is whether meeting one market's rules automatically clears another. It does not. Maximum residue levels and food standards are set by each jurisdiction, and they are not globally harmonized.

A residue profile that passes in one country can fail in another. Different authorities may set different limits for the same substance, and may apply different default rules where a specific limit is absent. This is why experienced sellers treat each destination as a separate compliance exercise rather than assuming one test report works everywhere.

That said, the underlying logic is similar across markets. Authorities set limits, importers must comply, and enforcement happens at the border or in the domestic market. Understanding who does what in each place is the first step.

Australia and New Zealand: FSANZ and the Food Standards Code

For Australia and New Zealand, the relevant body is Food Standards Australia New Zealand, commonly shortened to FSANZ. FSANZ develops the joint Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code, which applies in both countries.

Pesticide maximum residue limits in that code are set out in Schedule 18, often written as S18. If you are preparing a tea shipment for either market, Schedule 18 is the reference point for residue limits. Imported food must comply with the applicable requirements in the Food Standards Code.

A few practical points follow from this:

  • Check the current Schedule 18. Like most residue schedules, it is updated over time. An old copy should not be relied on.
    • Match the commodity correctly. Tea is a specific commodity; make sure you are looking at the right entry, not a generic "herbs" or "spices" line.
      • Check each substance. MRLs are set per pesticide, not as a single blanket number for tea.
        • Work from the official code. Use the official Food Standards Code published by FSANZ rather than a third-party summary.

          • The big-picture habit is the same one that serves buyers well in the EU: identify the exact standard, identify the exact substance, and look up the current value. Our article on [EU MRLs for tea](/posts/what-are-eu-mrls-for-tea/) demonstrates the same lookup discipline in an EU context.

          Canada: Health Canada sets limits, CFIA enforces

          In Canada, the roles are split between two bodies. Health Canada sets pesticide maximum residue levels under the Pest Control Products Act. CFIA — the Canadian Food Inspection Agency — is the enforcement side.

          For a tea importer, the practical rule is straightforward: food imported into Canada must comply with Canadian MRLs. That means checking the Canadian limit for the substances of concern on the product you are shipping, rather than assuming an EU or US value will apply.

          Because this is an overview, the advice here is deliberately general. Specific tea MRLs in Canada should be verified against the official Health Canada resources and the current Canadian MRL database. Values change, and quoting a number from memory is a reliable way to get a shipment into trouble.

          A sensible approach for a new exporter looks like this:

          • Identify the active substances used in the tea, using your supplier's records and past COAs.
            • Look up the Canadian MRL for each substance on the relevant commodity.
              • Check whether a default applies where no specific MRL exists, following the current Canadian rules.
                • Confirm documentation — test reports, lot identity, and supplier records — is available for the shipment.
                  • Check current official guidance before shipping, since requirements and administrative details can change.

                    • How this compares with the EU approach

                    If your baseline is the EU, one concept is worth carrying over. Under Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, the EU applies a default of 0.01 mg/kg where no specific MRL exists in Annexes II or III, or where an active substance is not listed in Annex IV. It is a legal compliance limit at the LOQ level — not zero residue and not a safety threshold. We explain the details in [the EU 0.01 mg/kg default limit](/posts/eu-default-limit-0-01-mg-kg/).

                    Do not assume another market uses the same default. Each authority sets its own rules. Some markets may apply a different default where a specific MRL is absent; others may treat the situation differently. The safe move is always to check the current official text for the destination market rather than carrying an EU number across by habit.

                    The same caution applies to how test reports are read. A "not detected" result still depends on the method's detection capability, and an LOD or LOQ that is too high can hide a residue that matters in a stricter market. Sampling is statistical too — one clean sample does not prove an entire lot is compliant. Our article on [tea batch testing limits](/posts/tea-batch-testing-limits/) explains why.

                    A note on broader food safety duties

                    Residue limits are only one part of importing food. Across markets, importers are generally expected to have systems in place for verifying suppliers and managing known hazards — whether that is documented in a US FSVP, a HACCP-based approach, or other domestic requirements.

                    In the United States, for example, the FSVP rule requires a written hazard analysis covering biological, chemical, and physical hazards, and a documented supplier evaluation. While the specific legal duties differ by country, the underlying expectation is similar: know your supplier, know the hazards, and keep records. If you are building a compliance system for one market, much of the documentation discipline will carry over to others — even if the legal citations do not.

                    For a structured view of the US side, see [FDA tea import rules](/posts/fda-tea-import-rules/) and [FSVP for tea importers explained](/posts/fsvp-tea-importers-explained/).

                    Practical first steps for a new market

                    If you are about to ship tea to Australia, New Zealand, or Canada for the first time, keep the process simple.

                    • Name the destination precisely. Rules differ even between close markets.
                      • Find the official body. FSANZ for Australia and New Zealand; Health Canada and CFIA for Canada.
                        • Get the current standard. Schedule 18 for AU/NZ; the Canadian MRL framework for Canada.
                          • Match substances to limits. One pesticide at a time, on the correct commodity.
                            • Keep records. COAs, lot numbers, supplier details, and correspondence.
                              • Verify before shipping. Recheck the official source close to the shipment date.

                                • Bottom line

                                Australia and New Zealand use the joint Food Standards Code developed by FSANZ, with pesticide MRLs in Schedule 18; Canada has Health Canada setting MRLs under the Pest Control Products Act and CFIA enforcing them. Imported tea must comply with the destination market's own limits — do not assume EU or US values carry over, and do not rely on quoted numbers from memory. Verify each substance against the current official standard, keep solid lot-level documentation, and treat every market as its own compliance exercise.
