EU & US Tea Standards · Buying Guide

Checklist: Safe Tea Compliance For EU And US Buyers

Buying tea across borders involves a surprising amount of paperwork, and the rules are different on each side of the Atlantic. This checklist pulls the essentials into one place: what to verify, what documents to hold, and which questions to ask before goods ship.

It is deliberately practical and deliberately general. It does not quote specific numeric limits for tea contaminants, because those belong in current official texts. Where a number matters, the instruction is to check the official source — not to trust a figure copied into a checklist.

Step 1: Identify the product and the market precisely

Before anything else, write down two things: exactly what you are buying, and exactly where it is going.

  • Commodity: Tea is a defined commodity in EU rules — dried leaves and stalks of *Camellia sinensis*, category 06, code 0610000. Make sure your product matches that definition.
    • Destination: EU and US requirements are separate. A report prepared for one is not automatically valid for the other.
      • Form: Dried leaf, instant tea, and flavored blends can raise different questions. Keep the description accurate.

        • Getting this step wrong makes every later step unreliable.

        Step 2: Check pesticide MRLs substance by substance

        There is no single "tea MRL" number. Limits are set per active substance, and the correct value depends on whether a specific MRL exists.

        • Under Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, specific MRLs are set in Annexes II and III. Where no specific MRL exists, Article 18(1)(b) applies a default of 0.01 mg/kg — a legal compliance limit at the LOQ level, not zero residue and not a safety threshold.
          • Tea is a commodity under Annex I (code 0610000), and many substances have their own specific MRLs. Look each one up.
            • Use the EU Pesticides Database to check values; the annexes are amended frequently, so check the current consolidated regulation.

              • For the deeper reasoning behind the default, read [the EU 0.01 mg/kg default limit](/posts/eu-default-limit-0-01-mg-kg/). For the framework itself, see [EU Regulation 396/2005 and tea](/posts/eu-regulation-396-2005-tea/) and [what are the EU MRLs for tea](/posts/what-are-eu-mrls-for-tea/).

              Step 3: Check contaminants and heavy metals

              Pesticides are not the only concern. EU contaminant maximum levels are set in Regulation (EC) No 1881/2006, which covers mycotoxins, metals such as lead, cadmium, mercury and arsenic, and other contaminants.

              • Food above the applicable annex limits may not be sold.
                • The regulation includes a ban on mixing compliant food with non-compliant food — you cannot dilute a problem batch into clean stock.
                  • There is no single dedicated EU-wide lead limit specifically for tea leaves. Buyers rely on import controls, national enforcement, and their own COA testing. Do not accept a quoted "EU tea lead limit" without an exact legal citation.

                    • Sources of metals and how they are tested are covered in [lead and arsenic in tea](/posts/lead-arsenic-in-tea/) and [heavy metal sources in tea](/posts/heavy-metal-sources-tea/). For sampling and analysis of contaminants, Regulation (EC) No 333/2007 is the reference; measurement uncertainty is reported as x +/- U with a coverage factor of 2, roughly 95 percent confidence.

                    Step 4: Verify the lab report, not just the claim

                    A "tested" claim is only as good as the report behind it. A proper COA should identify the lab and its accreditation, the lot ID, sampling and report dates, analytes, method, result with unit, LOQ and LOD, and pass or fail against a stated limit.

                    • Match the report to the lot. A clean report for a different lot is not evidence about your goods.
                      • Check the scope. Were the relevant analytes tested, and were the detection limits low enough to matter in your market?
                        • Remember sampling is statistical. One good test does not prove every bag in a lot is compliant.

                          • Work through [how to read a tea lab test report](/posts/read-tea-lab-test-report/), [tea batch testing limits](/posts/tea-batch-testing-limits/), and [how tea samples are tested in EU labs](/posts/how-tea-samples-tested-eu-labs/). For finding a competent lab, see [third-party tea testing labs](/posts/third-party-tea-test-labs/).

                          Step 5: Set up your US FSVP before goods ship

                          For the United States, the Foreign Supplier Verification Program under 21 CFR Part 1 Subpart L is a core obligation for importers.

                          • Develop, maintain and follow a written FSVP giving adequate assurance the foreign supplier uses processes providing at least the same level of public health protection as US requirements.
                            • Complete a written hazard analysis covering biological, chemical (including pesticide and drug residues, natural toxins, unapproved additives and allergens) and physical hazards.
                              • Evaluate and approve the supplier, considering the hazard analysis, who controls the hazard, supplier performance, any warning letter or import alert or other FDA compliance action, and food safety history. Reevaluate at least every three years.
                                • Define verification activities in writing — onsite audits, sampling and testing, review of supplier records, or other appropriate activities — and document them.
                                  • Keep records for at least two years after they are created or obtained.

                                    • Verification work must be done by a qualified individual; onsite audits by a qualified auditor. You may not rely on the foreign supplier itself to perform verification activities, except for sampling and testing. If you are below the "very small importer" threshold (human food averaging less than $1 million a year over the prior three years), reduced requirements apply, but core duties still remain. Full detail is in [FSVP for tea importers explained](/posts/fsvp-tea-importers-explained/).

                                    Step 6: Get US prior notice and entry details right

                                    21 CFR Part 1 Subpart I governs prior notice. It applies to all food imported or offered for import, with limited exemptions.

                                    • Submit electronically to FDA, via ABI/ACE/ITDS or FDA PNSI, in English.
                                      • Timing: at least 2 hours before arrival by road, 4 hours by rail or air, and 8 hours by water; international mail requires prior notice before the article is sent to the US.
                                        • Submit required content: importer, manufacturer, shipper, product code, lot numbers, port and arrival details, and whether the food was previously refused by any country.
                                          • No, inaccurate or late prior notice can lead to refusal of admission.

                                            • Prior notice is separate from facility registration and separate from DWPE detention. The walk-through is in [tea prior notice with FDA](/posts/tea-prior-notice-fda/), and the broader picture in [FDA tea import rules](/posts/fda-tea-import-rules/) and [why tea fails US customs](/posts/why-tea-fails-us-customs/).

                                            Step 7: Screen for FDA import alerts

                                            FDA import alerts are enforcement tools that can place products under detention without physical examination (DWPE). There is no tea-specific import alert — tea is covered by general food, chemical and FSVP alerts, notably 99-41 (FSVP violations) and 99-48 (chemical contamination). Check the official FDA import alert list before shipping. See [FDA import alert and detention notices](/posts/fda-import-alert-detention/) for the red list, green list and what to do if goods are held.

                                            Step 8: Handle storage, claims and labeling carefully

                                            • Store tea cool, dry, sealed, away from light and odors, and rotate stock first-in, first-out. Poor storage can lead to mold and potential mycotoxin risk. See [mycotoxin risk in stored tea](/posts/mycotoxin-risk-stored-tea/) and [pu-erh storage and humidity](/posts/pu-erh-tea-storage-humidity/).
                                              • Do not make disease-treatment or health claims. Under the FD&C Act, a product intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat or prevent disease can be treated as a drug. Ordinary tea sold as food may not bear such claims. Our discussion of [tea labeling rules and health claims](/posts/tea-labeling-rules-us-health-claims/) treats this strictly as a legal labeling topic.
                                                • Be cautious with "zero residue" and "organic" claims. Neither is a guarantee of no measurable residue. See [zero pesticide residue meaning](/posts/zero-pesticide-residue-meaning/) and [organic vs conventional tea residue](/posts/organic-vs-conventional-tea-residue/).

                                                  • Quick checklist to keep beside your desk

                                                  • [ ] Commodity, form and destination clearly identified
                                                    • [ ] Each relevant pesticide checked against the current MRL in the destination market
                                                      • [ ] Contaminant requirements reviewed; no mixing of non-compliant stock
                                                        • [ ] COA matched to the exact lot, with scope and detection limits checked
                                                          • [ ] FSVP written, hazard analysis and supplier evaluation complete
                                                            • [ ] Prior notice submitted on time with accurate entry details
                                                              • [ ] Supplier screened against FDA import alerts
                                                                • [ ] Storage conditions and first-in-first-out rotation in place
                                                                  • [ ] No prohibited health claims on labels or listings
                                                                    • [ ] All records stored and retrievable for the required period

                                                                      • Bottom line

                                                                      Safe tea buying into the EU and US comes down to a handful of disciplines: check each substance against the current MRL, verify contaminants without inventing limits, demand a COA that matches the lot, maintain a written FSVP, submit accurate prior notice on time, screen for import alerts, store the tea properly, and keep clean labeling. None of it is exotic — but each step protects you from a shipment that stops at the border. Use the linked articles to go deeper on whichever step you need.
